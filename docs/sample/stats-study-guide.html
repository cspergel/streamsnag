From a sample to a claim: standard error, confidence intervals and p-values

Big picture

The lecture asks how a single sample can support a claim about a whole population. It first shows that the sample mean varies from sample to sample in a predictable way (the sampling distribution). That variation is measured by the standard error, which gives confidence intervals, with t for small samples. The same logic yields hypothesis tests and p-values, and the lecture ends with their misreadings, error types and multiple testing.

After this lecture you should be able to

  • explain the difference between a parameter and a statistic and describe the sampling distribution of the mean (centre, spread, shape)
  • calculate a standard error and a 95% or 99% confidence interval from a sample mean, s and n, using t when the sample is small
  • distinguish standard deviation from standard error and predict how sample size changes the standard error
  • interpret a 95% confidence interval correctly, as a statement about the method and not about one interval
  • carry out a one-sample hypothesis test with z, decide whether to reject H0, and link the result to the confidence interval
  • compare type I and type II errors and power, and decide how to adjust alpha with a Bonferroni correction when running many tests

1

Why does the sample mean vary between samples, and how is that variation described?Core

01:35 · slide 2also at02:2403:0004:4505:0905:21

The sample mean varies because each survey picks different students, while the population mean stays fixed. The population is everybody the question is about, and its true average, mu, is a parameter: a fixed number you never learn exactly. The sample mean, x-bar, is a statistic and changes with every new sample. Repeat the survey thousands of times and draw a histogram of the means, not of students: that is the sampling distribution of the mean. It is centred on mu, so the sample mean is unbiased. Its spread is sigma over root n. Its shape is approximately normal, even if the population is not, by the central limit theorem.

In the lecturer's words“That group, everybody we care about, is the population, and the true average for that whole population gets a Greek letter, mu.”
Example

A survey of 64 students gave a mean of 6.8 hours, but a different 64 students might give 6.6 or 7.1, even though the true mean has not moved.

Ask yourself

Why can the sampling distribution of the mean be centred on mu and still be different from the distribution of individual students' sleep?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

Think of mu as a bullseye painted on a wall and each sample mean as one dart. Unbiased means the darts cluster around the bullseye, not that any single dart hits it. Sample size controls how tightly they cluster.

How does the standard error differ from the standard deviation, and how does sample size affect it?Core

07:36 · slide 4also at07:1607:4408:0108:25

The standard deviation measures how much individual people differ, while the standard error measures how much the sample mean would vary from survey to survey. The standard error is the standard deviation divided by the square root of n. Collecting more data does not shrink the standard deviation, because students are as varied as ever, but it does shrink the standard error. Since n sits under a square root, the gain is slow: to halve the standard error you need four times the data. Confusing the two is the most common exam mistake.

In the lecturer's words“The standard deviation, one point six hours, tells you how much individual students differ from each other.”
Example

With s = 1.6 hours and n = 64, SE = 1.6/8 = 0.2 hours, or 12 minutes. Going to 256 students gives 1.6/16 = 0.1 hours: four times the work for twice the precision.

Ask yourself

Why does collecting more data shrink the standard error but leave the standard deviation unchanged?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

The square-root law means returns diminish. The first extra participants sharpen your estimate a lot, later ones much less, so there is a point where more data stops being worth its cost.

How is a 95% confidence interval built, and what does '95%' actually refer to?Core

09:54 · slide 5also at09:2510:3011:1411:5012:10

A 95% confidence interval is the sample mean plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, and the 95% describes the method, not any single interval. The sampling distribution is normal, and in a normal distribution 95% of values fall within 1.96 standard deviations of the centre. So 95% of the time x-bar lands within 1.96 standard errors of mu, and so mu is within that distance of x-bar. Higher confidence widens the interval. Because mu is fixed, it is either in your interval or not; the random thing is the interval. Across repeated surveys, about 95 in 100 intervals would contain mu.

In the lecturer's words“the sample mean, plus or minus one point nine six times the standard error.”
Example

For the sleep survey, 1.96 × 0.2 ≈ 0.39, so the interval is 6.8 ± 0.39, or 6.41 to 7.19 hours. At 99% the multiplier is 2.576, giving 6.28 to 7.32.

Ask yourself

Why is it wrong to say there is a 95% chance the true mean lies between 6.41 and 7.19, and what does the 95% refer to instead?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

Think of a net thrown at a fixed fish. Your net works 95% of the time, but once it has landed, the fish is either under it or not. Confidence belongs to the throwing method.

Why do small samples need Student's t, and how does it change the interval?

14:18 · slide 7also at15:2315:3815:5616:13

Small samples need Student's t because the sample standard deviation s is a wobbly estimate of the true sigma, and ignoring that makes intervals too narrow, claiming more precision than the data justify. The t distribution has fatter tails, and its multiplier depends on the degrees of freedom, which for one sample is n minus one. A bigger multiplier widens the interval. As the sample grows, t settles towards the normal value, so for large samples it makes little difference. In practice people use t whenever sigma is unknown, which is always.

In the lecturer's words“With a small sample it is not, it is itself quite wobbly, and if we ignore that, our intervals come out too narrow.”
Example

With n = 16 and s = 1.6, SE = 0.4 and there are 15 degrees of freedom. The multiplier is 2.131 instead of 1.96, so the margin is about 0.85 and the interval runs 5.95 to 7.65 hours. At 30 degrees of freedom it is 2.042, and at 100 it is 1.984.

Ask yourself

How do the smaller sample and the larger t multiplier each contribute to the wider interval at n = 16 compared with n = 64?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

The t distribution is the price of not knowing sigma: extra uncertainty about the spread is paid for with heavier tails. When degrees of freedom are large, that extra uncertainty vanishes and t becomes the normal curve.

How does a hypothesis test turn the sample mean into a p-value and a decision?Core

18:12 · slide 8also at17:1717:4818:3319:3520:11

A hypothesis test measures how surprising your sample mean would be if a claim were true, turns that into a probability, and compares it with a threshold. First state the null hypothesis, the claim being tested. Then express the gap between sample mean and claim in standard errors: this is the test statistic. Then find the p-value: the probability, if the null were true, of landing that far or further away in either direction. Finally compare it with alpha, chosen in advance, conventionally 0.05. If p is below alpha, reject the null; otherwise you fail to reject, which is not proof, since absence of evidence against is not evidence for.

In the lecturer's words“If the null were true, how often would a sample land three and a half standard errors away or further, in either direction?”
Example

The leaflet claims mu = 7.5 hours. The sample gives (6.8 − 7.5)/0.2 = −3.5 standard errors, a p-value of about 0.0005 (five in ten thousand), so the null is rejected. For a claim of 7 hours, the distance is one standard error, p ≈ 0.32, so we fail to reject.

Ask yourself

Why do we say we fail to reject a null of 7 hours rather than saying we have proved the mean is 7, and how does this match the confidence interval?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

A p-value is not the probability that the null is true. It assumes the null and asks how surprising the data are. Also, a tiny p-value says the effect is detectable, not that it is large; the interval shows size.

Review sheets

Building and interpreting a confidence interval

A confidence interval turns one sample mean into a range for the true mean of all students, built from the standard error and a multiplier such as 1.96 for 95%. The rest of the lecture needs it because the '95%' wording is the most commonly misinterpreted statement in the course and loses exam marks.

Step by step

  1. First get the standard error. In the sleep survey the standard deviation was 1.6 hours and n was 64, whose square root is 8. So 1.6 divided by 8 is 0.2 hours, which is 12 minutes. 07:16
  2. The standard error of 0.2 hours says how much the sample mean would differ from survey to survey. The standard deviation of 1.6 hours says how much individual students differ. Mixing these two up is the most common exam mistake. 07:44
  3. The sampling distribution is normal, and in a normal distribution 95% of values fall within 1.96 standard deviations of the centre. So 95% of the time the sample mean lands within 1.96 standard errors of the true mean. That is the basis of the interval. 09:25
  4. If the sample mean is within that distance of the true mean, the true mean is within the same distance of the sample mean. So a 95% interval is the sample mean plus or minus 1.96 times the standard error. 09:25
  5. For the survey, 1.96 times 0.2 is 0.392, about 0.39. The interval for the mean sleep of all students is 6.8 ± 0.39, so 6.41 to 7.19 hours. The plus-or-minus part, 0.39, is the margin of error. 09:54
  6. For more confidence, use a 99% interval. The multiplier rises from 1.96 to 2.576, and 2.576 times 0.2 is about 0.52. The interval becomes 6.28 to 7.32 hours: more confidence gives a wider interval. 10:53
  7. Now the meaning of 95%. The true mean is a fixed number, either inside this interval or not; the interval is the random thing, since it comes from a random sample. So 95% describes the procedure: of 100 repeated surveys, about 95 intervals would contain the true mean and about 5 would miss. 12:10

How it connects

The standard error step links to the lecture's idea of how the standard error differs from the standard deviation. The interval's meaning of '95%' is the lecture's idea of what 95% actually refers to.

Common mistakes

  • MistakeTreating the standard error and the standard deviation as the same number.InsteadThe standard deviation (1.6 hours) is about how individual students differ; the standard error (0.2 hours) is about how the sample mean varies between surveys.
  • MistakeSaying there is a 95% chance the true mean lies between 6.41 and 7.19.InsteadThe true mean is fixed, either in the interval or not. The 95% describes the procedure, not this one interval.
  • MistakeThinking the true mean is the random quantity that jumps around.InsteadThe true mean is fixed; the interval is random because it comes from a random sample.
  • MistakeExpecting higher confidence to come with the same interval width.InsteadMore confidence means a wider interval: the multiplier rises from 1.96 to 2.576, so 99% gives 6.28 to 7.32 hours.

From test statistic to p-value, and what p does not mean

This topic is the four-step procedure that turns a sample mean into a p-value and a decision about a claim, together with what the p-value does and does not tell you. The rest of the lecture needs it because it is the formal use of the standard error and links testing to confidence intervals.

Step by step

  1. A test begins by stating the null hypothesis, the claim being tested. In the sleep example the leaflet says students get seven and a half hours a night, so the null is that the mean mu equals 7.5. 17:17
  2. Next ask how surprising the data would be if that claim were true, measured in standard errors. The sample mean is 6.8, the claim is 7.5, so the difference is minus 0.7, and the standard error is 0.2. 17:17
  3. Divide the difference by the standard error: minus 0.7 divided by 0.2 is minus 3.5. This is the test statistic, here a z score. The sample mean lies 3.5 standard errors below what the leaflet claims. 17:48
  4. Turn the test statistic into a probability. If the null were true, how often would a sample land 3.5 standard errors away or further, in either direction? The answer is about 0.0005, five times in ten thousand, and this is the p-value. 18:12
  5. Compare p with a threshold alpha chosen in advance, by convention 0.05. Here p is far below it, so the null is rejected: the data are not compatible with 7.5 hours. 18:33
  6. The p-value is the probability of data at least as extreme as yours, if the null is true. It starts by assuming the null, so it is the probability of the data given the hypothesis, not of the hypothesis given the data. p = 0.03 does not mean a 3% chance the leaflet is right. 20:50
  7. A small p does not mean a big effect, since it depends on effect size and sample size. With 10,000 students and a mean only 0.05 hours (3 minutes) below the claim, SE is 1.6/100 = 0.016, z is about 3.1 and p about 0.002. 21:58

How it connects

The test statistic is built from the standard error, which depends on sample size (with 10,000 students, SE = 1.6/100 = 0.016). The test also mirrors the 95% confidence interval: the interval is exactly the set of values a 5% level test would not reject.

Common mistakes

  • MistakeA p-value of 0.03 means there is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true.InsteadThe p-value is the probability of the data given the null, not the probability of the hypothesis given the data.
  • MistakeA very small p-value means the effect is big or important.Insteadp depends partly on effect size and partly on sample size; 3 minutes of sleep can be highly significant with 10,000 students.
  • MistakeA p-value above 0.05 shows there is no effect.InsteadIt may just mean the sample was too small to detect the effect.
  • MistakeTreating the test and the confidence interval as unrelated results that could disagree.InsteadThe 95% interval is exactly the set of values a 5% level test would not reject: 7.5 lay outside and was rejected, 7 lay inside and was not.

    A team runs 20 independent tests at alpha = 0.05 and none of the effects are real. They then switch to a Bonferroni correction. What happens, and why?

    Show answer

    Parameter 01:35 · slide 2
    The population is everybody the question is about, and its true average is called mu, which is a parameter.
    Power 24:18 · slide 10

    Sampling distribution of the mean 04:45 · slide 3

    Statistic 02:24 · slide 2

    Statistical significance 22:56 · slide 9

    Type I error 23:37 · slide 10
