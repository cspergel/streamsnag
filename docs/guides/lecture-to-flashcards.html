How to turn a lecture into flashcards

At the end you have a set of cards worth reviewing, each pointing back to its minute, ready for StreamSnag's review or for Anki. By hand with no AI, or with a Pro study pack written by your own AI account.

Most flashcards fail because they are badly written, not because flashcards do not work. Here are the rules of a good card, a way to write them by hand, and how StreamSnag writes and checks them for you.

The rules of a good card

The example cards here are about an invented lecture on cardiac output, the one our sample study pack was made from. For a larger deck, see the flashcards of the law sample.

  1. One fact per card

    If a card asks for two things, you will often know one and miss the other, and then you cannot mark it honestly. Split it.

    WeakWhat are the determinants of stroke volume and how does each one change it?Three facts and three directions in one card.
    BetterAfterload: what happens to stroke volume when afterload rises with preload and contractility unchanged?Answer: stroke volume falls.

  2. A short question and a shorter answer

    If the answer runs past a line, it is more than one card. As a working limit, keep the question under 25 words and the answer under 12.

  3. Say what the card is about

    Open with the topic, then a colon. A month from now the card will come up on its own, with no lecture around it.

    WeakWhat happens when it increases?What is “it”?
    BetterFrank-Starling law: what happens to stroke volume when end-diastolic volume increases?Answer: stroke volume increases.

  4. Ask for recall, not a guess

    Avoid true or false, yes or no, and “which of the following”. You can get those right without knowing the answer. Make yourself produce it.

  5. No lists

    A card that says “name all five” fails when you miss one, every time. Ask for one item, or hide one item and show the rest.

    WeakList the three determinants of stroke volume.Miss one and the whole card is wrong.
    BetterStroke volume: set by three things, namely preload, afterload and [...].Answer: contractility. One thing is hidden.

  6. Do not give the answer away

    Read the question and check that the answer is not sitting in it. Give numbers their units: “5” is not an answer, “5 litres a minute” is.

  7. Reword it, do not copy it

    A fill-in-the-blank lifted straight from the transcript tests whether you remember the sentence. Put the idea in new words and you test the idea.

  8. Keep the source

    Note the minute, the slide and the lecturer's words on every card. When a card stops making sense, you can go back and see what was meant.

  9. Make fewer cards

    Every card is a promise to review it many times. About 24 cards for an hour of lecture is a good set for most lectures. Pick what you would be sorry to forget.

Doing it by hand

No AI is needed for this.

  1. Get the transcript

    Working from text is much faster than pausing a video. See how to get a transcript of a lecture. Saving a lecture's captions as a transcript is free.

  2. Mark the ideas, not the sentences

    Read through once and mark what the lecturer stressed, repeated or said you need to know. Skip the housekeeping and the stories.

  3. Write one card per idea

    Follow the rules above. Add the minute from the transcript to each card.

  4. Cut

    Go through the set once more and delete every card you would not mind forgetting.

With a StreamSnag study pack

A study pack does the same job and applies the same rules. It is part of Pro, and it is written by your own AI account: a Claude or ChatGPT subscription you already have, or a pay-as-you-go key. The guide and quiz that come with the cards are covered in how to make a study guide and practice quiz from a lecture.

  1. Save the lecture and its transcript

    The pack is written from the transcript and the text of the slides. If the lecture has no captions, Pro can transcribe it on your computer first.

  2. Choose an AI account

    AI is off until you pick one in Settings, on the AI tab. It works with a Claude or ChatGPT subscription, or a key for Anthropic, OpenAI, Gemini, DeepSeek or OpenRouter.

  3. Press Make study pack

    It is the fourth step on the lecture's path, which “Show the path” opens. Before anything is sent, a sheet headed “Before the AI reads this lecture” shows what it will make, what it sends (the transcript, and the text and speaker notes of that lecture's slides, never slide pictures or the video), to which account, and roughly how much AI use the run will take. You choose which parts to make there, and the number of flashcards in Settings.

  4. Let it read, write and check

    It reads the lecture section by section and pulls out facts with the lecturer's exact words. Cards are written only from those facts. Then every card is checked against the rules above, and the ones that break a rule are dropped. A last pass removes anything the lecture does not support. It takes one to three minutes and carries on if you close the popup.

In the cardiac sample, 21 cards were written and 11 were kept. Nine were over the number asked for and one pointed at something the card did not show.

What the AI costs

AI use is never part of the StreamSnag price, and is never paid to StreamSnag. With a Claude or ChatGPT subscription you already have, a pack costs nothing extra: the run counts toward your plan's limits, and a plan can run out part-way through a busy day. With a pay-as-you-go key, on our own runs a study pack for a 64-minute lecture took about 30 requests and cost about US$1.50 on Claude Sonnet and US$0.04 to 0.08 on DeepSeek Flash; a 28-minute lecture cost about US$0.80 on Claude Sonnet. Google Gemini has a free daily allowance and then charges your Google account. A free allowance can do part of a pack and may run out part-way. More on AI costs.

Reviewing the cards

Reviewing is part of Pro and uses no AI. StreamSnag has its own review screen, opened by Flashcards at the foot of the popup. It shows what is due across all your lectures, a few new cards a day, and brings the cards you have seen back on their own days. Your progress stays on your computer. The cards come back over the coming days, with the due cards from every lecture in one place.

Every card has a Show me in the lecture button that opens the video at that minute and slide. If you use Anki, each study pack also saves a flashcards file: in Anki choose File, then Import, and pick that file. Here is the file from the cardiac sample.

Good to know

  • AI-written cards can be wrong. The checks reduce mistakes. They do not remove them. Every card points to its minute, so check anything important against the lecture itself.
  • Check your course's rules. Some universities restrict putting lecture material into AI tools. Whether to do it on a given course is your choice and your responsibility. Writing the cards by hand needs no AI at all.
