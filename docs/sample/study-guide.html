Big picture

This lecture asks how much blood the heart pumps and what sets it. Cardiac output is heart rate times stroke volume, and stroke volume depends on preload, afterload and contractility. Preload acts through the Frank-Starling mechanism, afterload through wall stress, and contractility through calcium. The pressure-volume loop shows all three at once, and exercise and heart failure apply them.

After this lecture you should be able to

  • calculate cardiac output, stroke volume and ejection fraction from heart rate, end-diastolic volume and end-systolic volume
  • explain why a normal resting cardiac output can still hide a failing ventricle
  • explain how the Frank-Starling mechanism works and why it keeps right and left ventricular outputs matched
  • predict how stroke volume and end-systolic volume change when preload, afterload or contractility is altered
  • compare a change in preload (movement along a curve) with a change in contractility (a new curve)
  • sketch the left ventricular pressure-volume loop and decide how each determinant changes it, and apply this to exercise and heart failure

Key ideas

1

How do heart rate and stroke volume set cardiac output, and what does ejection fraction add?Core

01:18 · slide 2also at01:3201:4101:5302:5303:16

Cardiac output is the volume one ventricle pumps per minute, and you get it by multiplying heart rate by stroke volume. Heart rate is beats per minute; stroke volume is the volume ejected in a single beat, which is end-diastolic volume (the volume in the ventricle after filling) minus end-systolic volume (what is left after contraction). Ejection fraction adds the share of filled volume that is ejected: stroke volume divided by end-diastolic volume. Normal is above about 55%, and below about 40% is called reduced, which is how heart failure is classified. Because output is rate times stroke volume, any change in output must come from heart rate, stroke volume, or both.

In the lecturer's words“Cardiac output is the volume of blood one ventricle pumps per minute.”
Example

At rest, 70 beats per minute times 70 mL gives 4,900 mL per minute, roughly 5 L/min. With an end-diastolic volume of 120 mL and an end-systolic volume of 50 mL, stroke volume is 70 mL and ejection fraction is 70/120, about 58%.

Ask yourself

Why must any change in cardiac output come from heart rate, stroke volume, or both, and how do end-diastolic and end-systolic volume feed into stroke volume?

2

Why can a normal cardiac output of 5 L/min still mean a failing heart?

04:43 · slide 2also at03:3203:5604:2505:14

A normal cardiac output only tells you the product of heart rate and stroke volume; it does not tell you how the heart reaches it. A failing ventricle can hold output at 5 L/min by beating faster while each beat is weak. Ejection fraction exposes this, because it shows how much of the filled volume is actually ejected. So you must look at how the output is achieved, not just the number. The reverse also happens: a low heart rate need not mean low output if each beat ejects more.

In the lecturer's words“So the lesson is that a normal cardiac output at rest does not mean a normal heart.”
Example

A patient with heart rate 100, end-diastolic volume 150 mL and end-systolic volume 100 mL has stroke volume 50 mL and output 5 L/min, which looks normal. But ejection fraction is 50/150, 33%, a badly failing ventricle. An athlete at 45 beats per minute with stroke volume near 110 mL also gets about 5 L/min.

Ask yourself

Why can two people with the same 5 L/min cardiac output have completely different heart health, and what measure reveals the difference?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

Think of two delivery vans carrying the same daily load: one makes a few big trips, the other many tiny ones with a struggling engine. The total looks identical, but only the total hides the strain. Output is a total; ejection fraction and heart rate show the effort.

3

Why does a larger end-diastolic volume give a larger stroke volume, and what is that for?Core

08:00 · slide 4also at08:3810:2912:2412:3813:04

A larger end-diastolic volume gives a larger stroke volume because the ventricle ejects more when it is filled more; this is the Frank-Starling law. Filling stretches the muscle cells, and that stretch is preload. Stretching the sarcomere makes troponin C more sensitive to calcium, so the same amount of calcium produces more force. This is intrinsic: no nerves or hormones are needed. Its main job is to keep the two ventricles' outputs matched. They are in series, so if the right ventricle pumps more, more blood returns to the left ventricle and its stroke volume rises on the next beat.

In the lecturer's words“Preload is the stretch on the ventricular muscle cells at the end of diastole, just before contraction.”
Example

If end-diastolic volume rises from 120 to 140 mL and end-systolic volume stays near 50 mL, stroke volume goes from 70 to 90 mL on the very next beats. A mismatch of even 1 mL per beat at 70 beats per minute would pile up 70 mL every minute in the lungs.

Ask yourself

Why does the Frank-Starling mechanism not count as a change in contractility, even though the ventricle pumps more?

4

How do afterload and contractility each change stroke volume, and how do they differ from preload?Core

06:25 · slide 3also at15:1015:1715:3418:1118:35

Afterload and contractility both change stroke volume, but through different routes. Afterload is the load the ventricle pushes against. With preload and contractility unchanged, higher afterload means the ventricle cannot empty as well, so more blood is left behind: end-systolic volume rises and stroke volume falls. Strictly, afterload is wall stress during ejection, not pressure itself. Contractility is the strength of contraction at a given preload and afterload, so a true rise gives a bigger stroke volume at the same end-diastolic volume. It usually comes from more calcium available to the filaments. Preload moves you along the same curve; contractility moves you onto a higher one.

In the lecturer's words“Three things, and you need to know these three cold. Preload, afterload, and contractility.”
Example

Noradrenaline from the sympathetic nervous system binds beta one receptors on ventricular muscle cells and raises contractility, giving a new curve shifted up and to the left. At any end-diastolic volume, stroke volume is higher, end-systolic volume falls and ejection fraction rises.

Ask yourself

How can you tell from a stroke volume graph whether a rise in stroke volume came from more preload or from more contractility?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

Preload is like how far you pull back a catapult; contractility is how strong the elastic is. Pulling further launches further along the same elastic, while stronger elastic launches further from the same pull.

5

How does the pressure-volume loop show the three determinants, and how do exercise and heart failure use them?

23:57 · slide 8also at23:0224:0524:2424:3625:03

On the pressure-volume loop, each determinant shifts the loop in a recognisable way, and the loop's width is stroke volume, end-diastolic minus end-systolic volume. More preload moves the bottom right corner right and widens the loop. More afterload makes the ventricle stop ejecting sooner, so the loop is taller and narrower with higher end-systolic volume. More contractility lowers end-systolic volume and widens the loop. The slope of the end-systolic pressure-volume line measures contractility. Exercise uses every knob together, while heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is basically a contractility problem, leaving the patient on a lower, flatter curve.

In the lecturer's words“you need to know that its slope means contractility”
Example

In hard exercise a healthy young adult's cardiac output rises from 5 to about 20 L/min, a fourfold increase. Heart rate goes from 70 to maybe 180, and stroke volume from 70 to around 110 or 120 mL.

Ask yourself

Why does raising afterload narrow the pressure-volume loop while raising contractility widens it, even though both change end-systolic volume?

Review sheets

Frank-Starling mechanism and matching of the two ventricles

The Frank-Starling mechanism is the heart's own rule that a ventricle filled with more blood during diastole ejects a bigger stroke volume on the next beat, and it works by making the filaments more sensitive to calcium. Its main job is to keep the outputs of the right and left ventricles matched, and the rest of the lecture, including contractility, builds on it.

Step by step

  1. Start with preload: the stretch on ventricular muscle cells at the end of diastole, just before contraction. This stretch cannot be measured in a patient, so end-diastolic volume, or sometimes end-diastolic pressure, is used as a stand-in. 08:00
  2. Now link preload to output. Raising end-diastolic volume makes the ventricle eject a bigger stroke volume: the more it is filled, the more it pumps out. This is the Frank-Starling law of the heart. 08:38
  3. On the graph, end-diastolic volume is on the x axis and stroke volume on the y axis. The curve rises steeply, then flattens at high volumes, because the muscle cannot be stretched indefinitely and keep giving more force. 09:16
  4. The response is intrinsic. In the lecturer's example, end-diastolic volume rises from 120 to 140 mL with end-systolic volume staying at about 50 mL, so stroke volume goes from 70 to 90 mL on the very next beats, with no nerves or hormones. 10:29
  5. Why does stretch give more force? Stretching sarcomeres from about 1.8 µm toward about 2.2 µm improves actin–myosin overlap, so more cross-bridges form. The bigger effect is that stretched troponin C becomes more sensitive to calcium (length-dependent activation). 12:08
  6. The calcium released does not change in this mechanism. Only the filaments' sensitivity to calcium changes, and the lecturer flags this as what separates it from contractility. 12:38
  7. The purpose is to keep the two ventricles' outputs matched. If the right side pumps more, more blood returns to the left ventricle, its end-diastolic volume rises, and its stroke volume rises on the next beat, so the sides stay equal beat by beat. 13:04

How it connects

The point that calcium amount stays constant is what separates this mechanism from contractility, which the lecture covers in a few minutes, alongside afterload. Stretch raises stroke volume on the next beat.

Common mistakes

  • MistakeThinking that stretch increases the amount of calcium released in the Frank-Starling mechanism.InsteadThe amount of calcium does not change; stretch makes the filaments, via troponin C, more sensitive to calcium.
  • MistakeThinking preload is a directly measured quantity in patients.InsteadStretch on a muscle cell cannot be measured in a patient, so end-diastolic volume or sometimes pressure is used as a stand-in.
  • MistakeAssuming stroke volume keeps rising in proportion as end-diastolic volume rises.InsteadThe curve rises steeply at first, then flattens at high volumes, because stretching further does not keep giving more force.
  • MistakeThinking the Frank-Starling response depends on nerves or hormones.InsteadIt is a property of the muscle itself, called intrinsic regulation; nothing nervous and no hormones are involved.

Pressure-volume loop and the three determinants

The left ventricular pressure-volume loop plots one heartbeat as a single anticlockwise trip, with volume on the x axis and pressure on the y axis. It matters because preload, afterload and contractility each change the loop's shape in a different way, so the loop shows all three determinants of stroke volume in one diagram.

Step by step

  1. The diagram is the pressure-volume loop of the left ventricle, with volume on the x axis and pressure on the y axis. One heartbeat is one trip anticlockwise round the loop, so the loop is the unit for reading everything that follows. 21:29
  2. Two things can be read off the loop. Its width is the stroke volume, end-diastolic minus end-systolic volume, which is 120 minus 50 mL in the lecturer's example. The area inside it is the stroke work, the work the ventricle does in one beat. 23:02
  3. Take the end-systolic point, the top left corner, from several loops at different filling volumes. Those points fall on a straight line, called the end-systolic pressure-volume relationship. Its slope measures contractility: a steeper line means a more strongly contracting ventricle, and a positive inotropic agent makes it steeper. 23:35
  4. Now turn the first knob, preload. The ventricle fills more, so the bottom right corner moves to the right and the loop gets wider. Since width is stroke volume, stroke volume goes up. 24:05
  5. Turn the second knob, afterload. The aortic valve opens later, at a higher pressure, so the loop gets taller and narrower. The ventricle stops ejecting sooner, end-systolic volume is higher, and stroke volume goes down. 24:24
  6. Turn the third knob, contractility. The ventricle empties further and the left side of the loop moves to the left. End-systolic volume is lower, the loop is wider, and stroke volume goes up. This matches the earlier graph, where a positive inotropic agent lowers end-systolic volume and raises ejection fraction. 24:36

How it connects

The loop links back to the earlier stroke-volume graph: a positive inotropic agent gives a whole new curve shifted upwards and to the left, not a move along the same curve, which is what the loop's leftward shift of end-systolic volume shows. It also sets up the two closing situations, starting with exercise, which use the three knobs.

Common mistakes

  • MistakeThinking the stroke volume is the height of the loop, or that a taller loop means a bigger stroke volume.InsteadThe width of the loop is the stroke volume, end-diastolic minus end-systolic volume. Higher afterload makes the loop taller but narrower, so stroke volume falls.
  • MistakeTreating the end-systolic pressure-volume line as a measure of preload or afterload rather than contractility.InsteadThe slope of the end-systolic pressure-volume relationship means contractility. A steeper line is a more strongly contracting ventricle, and the lecturer said to know this for the exam.
  • MistakeSaying a positive inotropic agent moves the ventricle along the same stroke-volume curve, like preload does.InsteadPreload moves you along the same curve. Greater contractility gives a whole new curve, shifted upwards and to the left, with lower end-systolic volume.

  4. Go further

    The Frank-Starling mechanism works by length-dependent activation of the sarcomere. Suppose a drug raised calcium entry into the cell instead. Would that be the same mechanism? Explain using what changes in each case.

    Show answer

    No. In the Frank-Starling mechanism the amount of calcium is unchanged; stretch improves filament overlap and makes troponin C more sensitive to calcium. A drug raising calcium entry makes more calcium available to the filaments, which is a change in contractility, giving a higher curve rather than a move along the same curve.

    A good answer includes

    • More calcium available means contractility change
    • Contractility shifts to a higher curve

  6. Explain

    Explain why the lecturer says that strictly afterload is wall stress rather than aortic pressure, and how this explains the value of left ventricular hypertrophy in chronic high afterload.

    Show answer

    Wall stress equals pressure times radius divided by twice wall thickness (Laplace). Pressure is only one factor. In chronic high pressure, a thicker wall in hypertrophy increases the denominator, bringing wall stress back toward normal, so hypertrophy compensates for high afterload.

    A good answer includes
    • Wall stress = pressure x radius / (2 x thickness)
    • Thicker wall lowers wall stress
    • Hypertrophy is compensation for high afterload
