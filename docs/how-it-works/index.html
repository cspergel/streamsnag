Two things Pro does differently.

Many online tools that turn a recording into notes work on their own servers, count your minutes and hand back a summary. Here is what StreamSnag does instead.

  • ProNo AI account

    No minute limits: transcription runs on your own computer.

    A lecture with no captions is turned into text on your own machine. We meter nothing, because nothing comes to us: no minutes to buy, no monthly allowance, no upload. Transcribe one lecture or a whole term.

    The AI parts are different, and we say so plainly. The study guide, the quiz and the flashcards are written by your own AI account. That provider may have its own limits. If you already pay for a Claude or ChatGPT subscription there is nothing extra to pay; with a pay-as-you-go key the provider charges you for what you use. StreamSnag adds no limit of its own and takes no share.

  • ProNo AI account

    It keeps what was taught, and every paragraph leads back to the recording.

    The reading edition is the lecture, not a summary of it: the lecturer's own words, examples and qualifications, set out to be read. Beside every paragraph is its time, which opens that moment in the recording.

    • A contents list, with the minute each part starts.
    • Headings. From the slides when there are some. Without slides, a part is headed by its own most distinctive terms where some stand out, and by its number and minute where none do.
    • “Lecturer's emphasis” beside a sentence where the lecturer says that something matters.
    • A spoken “first… second…” set out as a numbered list.

    None of this rewords anything. Hesitation sounds and doubled words are taken out by rule, and that is all. See one made without slides.

Three determinants of stroke volume

06:18–13:58
06:18

Lecturer: Okay. So what sets stroke volume? Three things, and you need to know these three cold. Preload, afterload, and contractility. Preload is how much the ventricle is stretched just before it contracts. Afterload is the load it has to push against to get the blood out.

06:42

Lecturer: And contractility is how strongly the muscle contracts for a given stretch and a given load. The other word for contractility is inotropy, and you'll see that on the slide, positive ionotropic and negative ionotropic drugs. The way I think about it, and this is just an analogy, is throwing a ball. Preload is how far back you pull your arm. Afterload is how heavy the ball is. And contractility is how strong your arm is.

07:21

Lecturer: You can throw further by pulling back more, by picking a lighter ball, or by going to the gym. Three different things. And the reason I labour this is that every year in the exam about a third of you mix up preload and contractility, so we are going to go slowly through each one.

An excerpt, word for word. The whole sample has 49 paragraphs in 4 parts, and its minutes open the sample study page, which has no video.

In this excerpt the captions wrote “ionotropic” where the slide says inotropic. A reading edition made without AI keeps the words as they were captioned or transcribed, mistakes included. That is why every paragraph links to its minute.
