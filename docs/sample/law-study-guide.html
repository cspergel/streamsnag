Big picture

This lecture answers how the law decides that two people have reached an agreement, building the whole answer out of offer and acceptance. It starts from the objective test, then sorts statements into offers and invitations to treat using shop displays, windows, adverts and auctions, before showing how a unilateral offer like Carlill is accepted by performance. It then requires acceptance to mirror the offer, examines when acceptance counts, and closes with how an offer ends and a four-question method for spotting a contract.

After this lecture you should be able to

  • Explain why agreement is judged objectively and apply the reasonable-person test to a statement
  • Decide whether a given statement is an offer or an invitation to treat, using displays, shop windows, advertisements and auctions
  • Analyse whether an advertisement is a unilateral offer in the style of Carlill, and explain how it is accepted
  • Apply the mirror image rule to distinguish an acceptance from a counter-offer or a request for information
  • Determine when acceptance is effective for instant methods, by post, and for silence
  • Identify the four ways an offer ends and decide whether a revocation came in time

Key ideas

1

Why does the law judge agreement by outward words and conduct rather than inner meaning?Core

01:14 · slide 2also at00:5901:4401:5802:13

The law judges agreement objectively, meaning it asks what a reasonable person watching what you said and did would have understood you to mean, rather than what was really going on inside your head. Courts have said this for a very long time, with Smith v Hughes in 1871 usually given as the authority. The reason is that nobody can see inside anybody else's head: if you could get out of a deal by saying you said yes but secretly meant no, no contract would be safe. So if you behave in a way that would lead a reasonable person to think you were agreeing, and the other side relies on that, you are held to it.

In the lecturer's words“One side makes an offer, the other side accepts it, and at the moment of acceptance, not a second before, there is a contract.”
Example

If I could get out of a deal by saying, well, I said yes, but secretly I meant no, no contract would be safe.

Ask yourself

Why does judging agreement by outward words and conduct, rather than inner meaning, make contracts safer for both parties?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

Think of it like a referee watching the game, not reading minds: what matters is what your actions looked like from the outside, not what you secretly intended.

When is a statement only an invitation to treat rather than an offer?Core

03:41 · slide 3also at05:0605:1505:3105:4806:21

A statement is only an invitation to treat when it is an opening move, an invitation to make an offer, rather than a promise to deal with anyone who turns up. It says, I might be interested, come and talk to me. The courts sort statements this way: goods displayed on a shelf in a shop are an invitation to treat, so the customer makes the offer at the till and the shop accepts there (Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots, 1953). This keeps both sides free until the till: the customer can put the box back, and the shop cannot be forced to sell. The same idea applies to shop windows (Fisher v Bell, 1961), advertisements generally (Partridge v Crittenden, 1968), and auctioneer requests for bids, where each bid is an offer and the fall of the hammer accepts it.

In the lecturer's words“An invitation to treat is an invitation to make an offer. It is an opening move.”
Example

In Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots (1953), the display on the shelf was only an invitation to treat; the customer makes the offer at the till and the shop accepts it there, under the pharmacist's eye.

Ask yourself

Why does treating a shop display as an invitation to treat keep both the customer and the shop free until the till?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

An invitation to treat is like a shop window saying 'come in and make me an offer' — it opens the conversation but does not commit either side yet.

How can an advertisement be an offer to the whole world, and how is it accepted?Core

09:25 · slide 5also at08:0708:4009:5110:2310:33

An advertisement can be an offer to the whole world, which anyone can accept by doing what it asks, as in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company (1893). The company advertised that it would pay one hundred pounds to anyone who used the smoke ball three times a day for two weeks as directed and still caught influenza. The court rejected the argument that it was a mere sales puff by pointing to the thousand pounds deposited with its bank to back the promise, which a reasonable reader would take seriously. In an offer of this kind, the offeror has by its own words done away with the need to be told of acceptance; doing what was asked is the acceptance. This is called a unilateral offer: only one side makes a promise (if you do this, I will pay you), and the other side promises nothing, they just perform.

In the lecturer's words“this advert was an offer, made to the whole world, which anyone could accept by doing what it asked”
Example

Mrs Carlill bought a smoke ball, used it as directed, caught influenza, and asked for her hundred pounds; the Court of Appeal held the advert was an offer to the whole world, accepted by doing what it asked.

Ask yourself

How does a unilateral offer, like the one in Carlill, differ from an ordinary bilateral contract in the way it is accepted?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

A unilateral offer is like a reward poster: you do not write back to accept, you just do the thing asked and the reward is owed.

Why does a counter-offer kill the original offer but a request for information does not?Core

12:11 · slide 6also at12:3913:1813:51

A counter-offer kills the original offer because acceptance has to match the offer exactly, which people call the mirror image rule. If you reply with different terms, that is not an acceptance at all; it is a counter-offer, which is a new offer the other side can accept or reject, and it kills the original offer so you cannot go back to it. In Hyde v Wrench (1840), the offer of a thousand pounds had died when Hyde made his counter-offer of nine hundred and fifty, so his later acceptance failed and there was no contract. But not every reply that is not a plain yes is a counter-offer: in Stevenson v McLean (1880), the buyer's reply asking whether he could take delivery of iron over a few months was held to be only a request for information, so the offer remained open to be accepted. The question is always whether the reply turned the offer down and put new terms in its place, or was just asking.

In the lecturer's words“acceptance has to match the offer exactly. People call this the mirror image rule”
Example

In Hyde v Wrench (1840), Wrench offered to sell his farm for a thousand pounds; Hyde offered nine hundred and fifty, and when Wrench said no, Hyde wrote accepting the thousand, but the court held there was no contract because the original offer had died.

Ask yourself

How do you tell the difference between a counter-offer and a mere request for information when replying to an offer?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

A counter-offer is like slamming a door and opening a new one: the old deal is gone. A request for information just asks a question and leaves the door open.

When is acceptance effective, and when does it not need communicating?Core

15:15 · slide 7also at15:3116:2517:0017:3317:49

An acceptance generally has to be communicated to the offeror; it is not enough to decide privately that you accept, and silence is not acceptance, as in Felthouse v Bindley (1862). You cannot make somebody's silence into a yes just by saying so in your offer. With instant ways of talking, face to face or by telephone, acceptance counts when it is received, as the courts said in Entores v Miles Far East (1955). But there is one big exception, the postal rule: where it is reasonable to accept by post, acceptance is complete as soon as the letter is posted, not when it arrives (Adams v Lindsell, 1818). The contract can exist even if the letter is delayed or lost and never arrives (Household Fire Insurance v Grant, 1879). The courts put the risk of the post on the person who made the offer, because they chose to start the deal in a way that invited a reply by post, and they can protect themselves by saying they must actually receive the acceptance in writing.

In the lecturer's words“An acceptance generally has to be communicated. It is not enough to decide, privately, that you accept.”
Example

In Household Fire Insurance v Grant (1879), the acceptance letter was lost and never arrived, but the contract was still held to exist because acceptance was complete when posted.

Ask yourself

Why does the postal rule place the risk of a lost acceptance letter on the offeror rather than the offeree?

Beyond the lecture (not from your lecturer)

The postal rule is like sending a letter: once it is in the postbox, it is out of your hands, so the law treats it as done. The offeror took the risk by inviting a postal reply.

Offer versus invitation to treat: the objective test and its standard examples

This topic explains how the law decides, objectively, whether what was said or done amounts to an offer or only an invitation to treat, using the standard examples of shop displays, shop windows, advertisements and auctions. It matters because identifying an offer is the first step of the four-question method, and the exam repeatedly tests whether a display, window, advert or auction is an offer.

Step by step

  1. Start from the objective test: the law does not look inside your head, it asks what a reasonable person watching what you said and did would have understood you to mean. This standard was stated as long ago as Smith v Hughes in 1871. 01:44
  2. With that test in hand, distinguish the offer from the invitation to treat. An invitation to treat is an invitation to make an offer, an opening move that says I might be interested, come and talk to me; it is not a promise to deal with anyone who turns up. 03:41
  3. Apply this to a shop shelf. In Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots (1953) the Court of Appeal held that the display on the shelf was only an invitation to treat, so the customer makes the offer at the till and the shop accepts it there, under the pharmacist's eye. 05:06
  4. The practical reason is that if the display were the offer, picking a box up would be acceptance and you could not change your mind and put it back; the shop also could not refuse to sell. Treating the display as an invitation to treat keeps both sides free until the till. 05:31
  5. The same idea covers a shop window. In Fisher v Bell (1961) a shopkeeper charged with offering a flick knife for sale was acquitted because putting goods in the window is an invitation to treat, not an offer. 06:21
  6. Advertisements follow the general rule that an advertisement is an invitation to treat, as in Partridge v Crittenden in 1968, where the man who advertised wild birds in a magazine was not guilty of offering them for sale. 06:41
  7. Auctions work the same way: the auctioneer's request for bids is an invitation to treat, each bid is an offer, and the auctioneer accepts one of them by bringing the hammer down, so until the hammer falls a bidder can withdraw the bid. 07:27

Common mistakes

  • MistakeThinking the law asks what you secretly intended inside your head.InsteadThe law judges agreement objectively, by what a reasonable person watching what you said and did would have understood.
  • MistakeTreating a shelf display or shop window as an offer.InsteadGoods displayed on a shelf or in a window are an invitation to treat, not an offer.
  • MistakeSaying the shop makes the offer and the customer accepts it at the till.InsteadThe customer makes the offer at the till and the shop accepts it there.
  • MistakeTreating each bid at an auction as an acceptance.InsteadEach bid is an offer, and the auctioneer accepts one by bringing the hammer down.

The mirror image rule and the timing of acceptance

The mirror image rule and the timing of acceptance explains what counts as acceptance, why a counter-offer is not acceptance but a request for information can leave the offer open, and when acceptance becomes effective. The rest of the lecture needs this because it builds on the idea that the law judges agreement by outward words and conduct, and it shows how to tell an offer from a counter-offer or a request for information.

Step by step

  1. Acceptance must match the offer exactly, which is called the mirror image rule. If you reply with different terms, that reply is not acceptance at all but a counter-offer. 12:11
  2. A counter-offer does two things: it is a new offer that the other side can accept or reject, and it kills the original offer so you cannot go back to it. 12:39
  3. Hyde v Wrench (1840) shows the effect: the offer of a thousand pounds had died when Hyde made his counter-offer of nine hundred and fifty, so his later acceptance failed and there was no contract. 13:18
  4. But not every reply that is not a plain yes is a counter-offer. In Stevenson v McLean (1880), the buyer's reply asking whether he could take delivery over a few months was held to be only a request for information, so the offer remained open to be accepted. 13:51
  5. An acceptance generally has to be communicated to the offeror; privately deciding to accept is not enough, and silence is not acceptance. 15:15
  6. For instant methods such as face-to-face or telephone, the courts said in Entores v Miles Far East (1955) that acceptance counts when it is received. 17:00
  7. The postal rule is a big exception: where it is reasonable to accept by post, acceptance is complete as soon as the letter is posted, not when it arrives, so the contract can exist even if the letter is delayed or lost and never arrives. The courts put the risk of the post on the offeror, because they chose to start the deal in a way that invited a reply by post, but the rule does not apply if the offer requires actual receipt of the acceptance in writing (Holwell Securities v Hughes, 1974). 17:33

How it connects

This topic links to the question of why a counter-offer kills the original offer but a request for information does not: the claims distinguish replies that turn the offer down and put new terms in place from replies that are just asking. It also links to when acceptance is effective and when it does not need communicating, through the general requirement of communication, the silence point and the postal rule.

Common mistakes

  • MistakeThinking any reply that is not a plain yes is a counter-offer.InsteadA reply that is only a request for information does not reject the offer, so the offer remains open to be accepted.
  • MistakeThinking you can accept the original offer after making a counter-offer.InsteadA counter-offer kills the original offer, so there is nothing left to accept.
  • MistakeThinking that deciding privately to accept, or staying silent, creates a contract.InsteadAcceptance generally has to be communicated to the offeror; silence is not acceptance.
  • MistakeThinking acceptance by post is complete only when the letter arrives.InsteadWhere it is reasonable to accept by post, acceptance is complete as soon as the letter is posted, not when it arrives.
