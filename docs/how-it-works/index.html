  1. 7:13

    3 · Read the lectureProNo AI account

    Read it instead of watching it.

    StreamSnag transcribes the lecture, with no minute limits, or starts from its captions when it has them, as this one does. Then one button, Read the lecture, sets it out as a clean document: a contents list, headed parts, and a time beside every paragraph that opens that moment in the video.

    It keeps what was taught, in the lecturer's own words. It is not a summary. It is made on your computer in a few seconds, and this 27-minute lecture is a 14-minute read.

    More detail

    If the lecture has no captions, Pro turns the speech into text on your own computer, with no minute limits. Nothing is uploaded and no AI account is used. It needs the small helper program and a one-time download of about 135 MB. It is set up for English; a larger model covers 25 European languages. An hour of lecture took under a minute on our fast desktop, and an older laptop can need several minutes.

    It needs no AI account: the words are the lecture's own, with hesitation sounds taken out by rule. If you have chosen an AI account, Read the lecture asks each time whether to tidy the wording as well. That extra uses your AI account. It never summarises, and a part that fails its check is shown as transcribed.

Three determinants of stroke volume

06:18–13:58
06:18

Lecturer: Okay. So what sets stroke volume? Three things, and you need to know these three cold. Preload, afterload, and contractility. Preload is how much the ventricle is stretched just before it contracts. Afterload is the load it has to push against to get the blood out.

06:42

Lecturer: And contractility is how strongly the muscle contracts for a given stretch and a given load. The other word for contractility is inotropy, and you'll see that on the slide, positive ionotropic and negative ionotropic drugs. The way I think about it, and this is just an analogy, is throwing a ball. Preload is how far back you pull your arm. Afterload is how heavy the ball is. And contractility is how strong your arm is.

07:21

Lecturer: You can throw further by pulling back more, by picking a lighter ball, or by going to the gym. Three different things. And the reason I labour this is that every year in the exam about a third of you mix up preload and contractility, so we are going to go slowly through each one.

More detail: what it adds, what it leaves alone, and its limits

What the reading edition adds, without any AI

  • A contents list, with the minute each part starts.
  • Headings. From the slides when there are some. Without slides, a part is headed by its own most distinctive terms where some stand out, and by its number and minute where none do.
  • “Lecturer's emphasis” beside a sentence where the lecturer says that something matters.
  • A spoken “first… second…” set out as a numbered list.

None of this rewords anything. Hesitation sounds and doubled words are taken out by rule, and that is all. See one made without slides.

What you can do with it

  • Search it. Find where a term was explained in seconds.
  • Print it. It is laid out for paper as well as the screen.
  • Read it on your phone. It is one file that works offline.
  • Jump to the video. Any paragraph opens that minute of the lecture.

No minute limits

A lecture with no captions is turned into text on your own machine. We meter nothing, because nothing comes to us: no minutes to buy, no monthly allowance, no upload. Transcribe one lecture or a whole term. It needs the small helper program and a one-time download of about 135 MB.

The AI parts are different, and we say so plainly. The study guide, the quiz and the flashcards are written by your own AI account. That provider may have its own limits. If you already pay for a Claude or ChatGPT subscription there is nothing extra to pay; with a pay-as-you-go key the provider charges you for what you use. StreamSnag adds no limit of its own and takes no share.

Optional, with your own AI account: it can also tidy the wording, taking out false starts and repairing the punctuation. With an AI account chosen, it asks each time. That part runs on your own AI account: no extra charge on a Claude or ChatGPT subscription you already have, a charge from the provider with a pay-as-you-go key. It never summarises, and a part that fails its check is shown as transcribed.

Its limits

It is a Pro feature. Free saves a video's existing captions as a transcript, when the video has them. The reading edition, and transcription for lectures with no captions, are Pro.

Most words right, not all. It depends on the recording. Speech models of this size misspell unusual drug and technical names. Every paragraph links to its minute so you can check, and the optional AI tidy corrects misheard terms against the slides. The marks for the lecturer's emphasis and the spoken lists work on English lectures whose captions have punctuation.

In the excerpt above the captions wrote “ionotropic” where the slide says inotropic. A reading edition made without AI keeps the words as they were captioned or transcribed, mistakes included. That is why every paragraph links to its minute.
